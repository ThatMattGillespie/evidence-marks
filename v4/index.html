Evidence marks: showing how much of each source we actually read

A proposed set of citation icons and sidebar cards, made in response to user testing. The aim is that a reader can tell, without stopping, whether a conclusion rests on a full paper, an abstract, a dataset or the open web.

Testers could not tell whether conclusions came from full papers or truncated abstracts, whether old studies still apply, what "Treat with Caution" means, or whether "no research found" means none exists. Conclusions drawn from abstracts lower trust in the whole research.

Inside the library: a pageHow much of the page is written on tells you how much we read. Three lines for full text, one line for an abstract. A dataset is a page of bars. Everything in the library takes the accent colour.

Outside the library: something elseA globe for the open web and a dashed page for a citation we couldn't find. Both are muted and dashed, the same way web sources are styled today.

In a sentence

The box stays, because the box is what says "open this evidence." The icon comes first, so a reader meets the kind of source before the number. The number is small and raised like a footnote, so it reads as a reference rather than a count.

Reintegration programmes in Afghanistan stalled wherever security guarantees for former combatants were weak 1. Community-based reintegration held up better than cash-only packages 2, and cash transfers alone are rated as having mixed effects across studies 3.

How a card reads, top to bottom

01

Kind, on the left of the top edge

It moved from the right so the eye meets it before the title. It names the specific document type, such as "Empirical study" rather than "Paper", because a meta-analysis and a working paper carry different weight. How much we read follows after the dot.

02

Citation number, then title

The same boxed number the reader clicked in the answer, so the link between sentence and source is obvious. The word "Cited" is gone: it made the number read like a count.

03

Year and language, right under the title

Grey sans serif, so it reads as part of the title block rather than a separate label. Year first, so readers can judge whether an older study still applies, then the original language and whether we read a translation.

04

An asterisk when there is a caveat

Abstracts, web pages and missing sources get an asterisk after the title, and the caveat follows the year and language on the same line. Full sources get neither, so the asterisk becomes the signal. This replaces "Treat with caution", which testers couldn't interpret.

Open questions

These need data or decisions before the design can be built as shown.

Needs engineering

Do we know when something was translated?

The library stores each paper's language, but nothing records whether Peace Intel read it in translation. "Read in EN translation" depends on that.

Needs engineering

What do What Works records hold?

The study count, period and country lines on those cards are placeholders. They need checking against the real records.

Design, next round

Old studies

Should a source past a certain age get its own caveat note, and what age? The year is visible, but testers asked whether old studies still apply.

Design and agent

"No research found"

This wording comes from the agent's answer, not the sidebar. It should say whether the library has nothing on the topic or the search found nothing, and suggest what to try next.

Every title, year, figure and website in the examples is a placeholder for layout. The colours and type follow Peace Intel's Rev 58 tokens.
